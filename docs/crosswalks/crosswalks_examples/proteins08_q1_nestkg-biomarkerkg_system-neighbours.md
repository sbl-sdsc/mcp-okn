Crosswalk: nestkg × biomarkerkg on UniProt accession. BiomarkerKG records which proteins are measured as clinical biomarkers and for which disease, but nothing about the assembly a protein belongs to. NeST records the assembly but nothing clinical. Both use `http://purl.uniprot.org/uniprot/{acc}`, so it is a direct IRI match; disease labels come from ubergraph.

Research question: Which clinically used protein biomarkers sit inside a NeST cancer protein system, and what else is in that system — i.e. which un-assayed proteins are the biomarker's system neighbours?

**Why this answers the question.** The second table is a hypothesis generator that neither graph produces alone. BiomarkerKG says BCL2 and BCL2L1 are measured in pancreatic cancer; NeST says they sit in a five-protein apoptotic module with BAD and BAX, which BiomarkerKG does *not* record as biomarkers. The join therefore nominates BAD and BAX as candidate markers on a structural argument — same assembly, same disease context — rather than by keyword similarity. The complement case is the same shape: C3 and C5 are established markers, while the regulators CFB and CFH that act in the same NeST module are not in BiomarkerKG at all.

The BTG/CCR4-NOT row shows the opposite extreme and is the more honest one: 1 biomarker among 21 members. A large deadenylase complex is not a panel of candidate markers just because one member is measured — which is exactly why the system SIZE column matters when reading this table.

**Caveats.** (1) BiomarkerKG's disease context and NeST's cancer-type evidence are independent; co-membership in a system is a *structural* argument for candidacy, not evidence that a neighbour is measurable in blood or tissue. (2) ALB and C3 mark COVID-19 as well as cancers here — BiomarkerKG is not cancer-scoped, so the join includes non-cancer marker roles for proteins that happen to sit in NeST systems. (3) Nesting means one protein legitimately appears in several systems; pick the smallest containing system when reasoning about neighbours.
