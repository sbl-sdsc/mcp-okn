**The bridge is obsolete, and removing it fixes the timeout.** This crosswalk routed biomarkerkg's DOID through ubergraph's `skos:exactMatch` to reach rdkg's MONDO. biomarkerkg has since migrated its disease axis to MONDO, so the two graphs now name diseases identically and join directly — no third graph in the join path.

| Route | Shared diseases |
|---|---|
| MONDO direct (live) | **754** |
| DOID via ubergraph bridge (retired) | 9 |

754 matches this crosswalk's stored `verified_count` exactly. The old route now reaches 9 diseases — the DOID vestige left behind by the migration.

The performance problem was the bridge: joining an unbound `?s ?p ?doid` scan over biomarkerkg to ubergraph's full `exactMatch` relation and *then* to rdkg is three-way work that exceeds the operation limit. Shrinking biomarkerkg's disease set in a `DISTINCT` subquery and joining once returns in about a second.

**The two routes are measured as separate queries on purpose.** Combining them in a single `UNION` re-introduces the cost of the retired branch and the whole query times out intermittently — it succeeded once and failed on re-run. Each route is cheap alone, so they are kept apart to make this transcript reliably reproducible rather than occasionally so.

**Why the join is required.** BiomarkerKG records which analytes are measured against a disease but nothing about causal genes or therapies; rdkg carries rare-disease gene and drug associations but no biomarker evidence. "Which diseases have both a measurable marker and a known genetic/therapeutic handle" exists only across the two.

**Why this answers the question.** These 754 diseases are where a clinician has both something to measure and something to act on — the intersection that makes a marker actionable rather than merely observed. The common cancers dominate because they are the best-studied on both axes at once.

**Two reading cautions, both visible in the table.** First, MONDO carries near-synonymous terms at different granularities and they appear as separate rows: `breast cancer` (1,086) and `breast carcinoma` (559), `colorectal cancer` (838) and `colorectal carcinoma` (712), `prostate cancer`/`prostate carcinoma`. Summing the column would double-count. Second, the top entry, **`hereditary disease` (1,528), is a container term**, not a disease — it absorbs rdkg's rare-disease assertions wholesale and tells you about the ontology's shape rather than about any patient.

So the useful reading is the *specific* terms, and even those rank by how heavily a disease is studied on both sides, not by how strong the biomarker–gene link is.
