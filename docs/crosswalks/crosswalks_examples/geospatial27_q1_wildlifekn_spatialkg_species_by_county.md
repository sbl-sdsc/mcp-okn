🧠 **Assistant**

**The break is a regex that assumed a three-part label.** The previous version extracted the county name from SpatialKG with `REPLACE(?rl,'^.*, ([^,]+ County), Florida.*$','$1')`. That pattern requires a comma *before* the county name. SpatialKG's county labels are two-part — `Alachua County, Florida` — so the pattern never matches, `REPLACE` returns the string unchanged, and the comparison `?county = ?cname` comes down to `"Alachua County" = "Alachua County, Florida"`, which is false for every row. Zero results, no error.

The fix is to build the SpatialKG label from the Wildlife-KN name rather than trying to tear it apart: `?spatialLabel = CONCAT(?county, ', Florida')`.

**But the bridge is weaker than it looks, and that is worth stating plainly.** Wildlife-KN's `Location` nodes carry **only two properties** — `rdf:type` and `rdfs:label`. There is no state, no FIPS, no coordinate. So a location labelled `Jackson County` cannot be resolved to a state from within Wildlife-KN at all.

That is **8.6 candidate counties per name** — county names repeat heavily across states. Ranking the name matches by state puts **Georgia (69) ahead of Florida (62)**, with Alabama, Mississippi, Iowa, Texas, Missouri and Tennessee close behind. Those are not competing claims about where the observations are; they are just evidence that the names are ambiguous.

**So the Florida scoping is an assumption, not a derivation.** The question asks about Florida, and restricting the join to `', Florida'` answers it — but the restriction comes from the question, not from the data. The earlier version of this transcript relied on the same assumption without stating it, which was safe when Wildlife-KN was Florida-only and is not safe now that it carries counties such as `Long County` (Georgia) and `Russell County` (Alabama).

**Why this answers the question.** The ranking is ecologically coherent: **Alachua** (Paynes Prairie, and the University of Florida's long observation record), **Wakulla** (St Marks NWR on the Gulf coast) and **Monroe** (the Keys and Everglades) are exactly the counties a Florida naturalist would expect near the top, and they sit alongside the large urban counties — Orange, Miami-Dade, Palm Beach, Hillsborough, Pinellas — where observer effort is highest.

That mixture is the caution. Species richness here is **observed** richness, so it measures birding effort as much as biodiversity. Orange County (Orlando) leading is more plausibly a statement about how many people record observations there than about it being Florida's richest habitat. The rural high-scorers — Wakulla at 73 with a fraction of the population — are the more interesting signal.

**Performance note.** Joining the 135 county names against SpatialKG's 6,228 county labels *while* also joining Wildlife-KN's observations times out: the observation fan-out multiplies the rows before the string comparison runs. Resolving the names in an inner subquery first, then joining back to the observations, brings it under a second.
