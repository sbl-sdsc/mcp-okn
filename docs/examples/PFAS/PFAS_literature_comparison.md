# Literature Comparison — PFAS Source-Attribution Knowledge-Graph Analysis

Each finding (F1–F6) from the analysis is checked against peer-reviewed literature, EPA/ATSDR/ITRC technical guidance, and primary site documentation (Maine DEP, EPA Superfund, National Guard/Air Force records, court filings). Claims that could not be independently verified within the search budget are labeled Unresolved rather than asserted. Bracketed numbers refer to the numbered reference list at the end of the file.

## F1 — Proximity gradient

**Verdict:** Corroborated

A national-scale analysis of EPA's UCMR3 drinking-water dataset found that the number of PFAS-manufacturing/using industrial sites, military fire-training areas, and wastewater treatment plants within a water system's watershed significantly predicted both PFAS detection frequency and concentration — each additional military site in a watershed was associated with a 20–35% increase in specific PFAS analytes among detectable samples [1]. Machine-learning models of private-well and public-supply PFAS occurrence consistently rank distance to the nearest point source (fire-training area, industrial facility, plastics/rubber/textile plant) among the top predictors, often outperforming hydrogeologic variables [2][3]. At finer, facility-adjacent scales, industrial-park and fluorochemical-plant studies report a clear spatial-attenuation pattern in soil and groundwater, and a 2025 European surface-water machine-learning study derived a "critical distance threshold" of roughly 4–5 km beyond which point-source influence becomes negligible, with the steepest gradient inside the first few kilometers [4][5]. A 2025 national inventory built specifically to profile PFAS site categories against groundwater detections likewise finds detections cluster spatially around known/presumptive source sites, and a contemporaneous Bayesian spatial model of California PFAS explicitly uses 1-km facility buffers (airports, chemical manufacturing, waste management) as predictors, confirming this is an accepted unit of analysis in the field [6][8]. The main caveat: most published proximity effects are demonstrated at watershed (HUC8) or multi-km buffer scales rather than validated cell-by-cell at exactly 1–3 km, and at least one small-sample facility study found a <2 km vs. ≥2 km difference that was not statistically significant — so gradient strength (not direction) is expected to vary by site and hydrogeology [7].

## F2 — Sector attribution

**Verdict:** Corroborated

Every sector in the analysis's list matches an EPA-designated or literature-documented dominant PFAS source category. EPA's Multi-Industry PFAS Study directly targeted organic-chemicals/plastics/synthetic-fiber (OCPSF) manufacturing, metal finishing (chromium electroplating flagged as the top contributor within that category), pulp/paper/paperboard manufacturing, textile mills, and commercial airports for PFAS effluent characterization, and separately names landfills and leather tanning as priority categories for revised effluent-limitations guidelines [9][11]. The National Academies' PFAS exposure guidance independently confirms AFFF/military firefighting, electroplating, tanneries, and wastewater/biosolids handling as established high-exposure sectors [10]. A 2025 national PFAS site-category inventory (2,219 confirmed + 79,891 presumptive sites) found AFFF-use sites had the highest average PFAS detections of any category and that metal-plating facilities were the single largest share (53%) of industrial contamination sites — both consistent with the analysis's top-ranked sectors [6]. The clearest sector **missing** from the list is fluorochemical/PFAS **manufacturing** itself — the plants that produce PFAS (fluoropolymer, fluorotelomer, and POSF-type production; e.g., Chemours Fayetteville Works, 3M) — which is EPA's own first and largest Multi-Industry Study category; **textile mills/carpet treatment** (also an explicit EPA category) is a secondary gap [9][6].

## F3 — Site validation

**Verdict:** Corroborated for site identity and contamination mechanism at all five locations; Partially corroborated for the specific maximum concentrations at three of the five (Bangor, Jay/Pixelle, and Tasman Leather/Hartland), where the site is a documented PFAS source but the exact cited figure could not be independently confirmed.

- **NAS Brunswick (claimed max 104,265 ng/L):** Corroborated. Brunswick is an active EPA Superfund/NPL site with decades of AFFF use [13]. Maine DEP monitoring wells have recorded PFOS up to 170,000 ppt and total PFAS >300,000 ppt in deep-overburden groundwater, and the August 2024 AFFF hangar-system spill (~5,500 L of concentrate) drove total PFAS in on-site stormwater to roughly 1.2 million ng/L, with elevated PFAS still measurable in downstream Harpswell Cove surface water 11+ days later [12][14]. The claimed maximum of 104,265 ng/L sits well within this documented range.
- **Bangor ANG/101st ARW & Bangor International Airport (claimed max 35,733–53,041 ng/L):** Partially corroborated. The site is a confirmed, actively investigated AFFF release site with Air National Guard/Air Force acknowledgment that contamination has migrated off-base into hundreds of surrounding private wells [15][16]. Publicly reported figures located here — PFOS 11,700 ppt at one 2018 source-area sample; PFOA 3,210 ppt / PFOS 58 ppt at another location — are lower than the claimed range. No public total-PFAS maximum matching 35,733–53,041 ng/L could be located; that specific figure is **Unresolved**, though the site's status as a major documented PFAS source is not in doubt.
- **Verso/Pixelle Androscoggin mill, Jay (claimed max 24,800 ng/L):** Partially corroborated. The Jay mill is a named defendant in Maine's PFAS paper-mill litigation over PFAS-containing biosolids (plaintiffs allege 46,234+ tons of biosolids from a related Verso facility alone), and DEP records show the mill land-applied 100,000+ cubic yards of waste/bio-ash regionally from the late 1980s–1990s [17][18]. No publicly reported concentration matching 24,800 ng/L could be located; Maine's statewide well table shows only one residential well tested in Jay itself (below the 20 ppt sum-of-6 standard), consistent with contamination being concentrated in mill wastewater/sludge/soil media rather than residential wells in Jay proper — the exact figure is **Unresolved** [23].
- **Tasman Leather Group + Hartland WWTF (claimed max 1,650 ng/L):** Partially corroborated. Leather tanning is an EPA-flagged PFAS-relevant sector, and Tasman's Hartland tannery discharges to the municipal Hartland WWTF under an NPDES permit [20]. That WWTF's effluent has been reported among the higher average PFAS concentrations of any Maine facility tested (~828 ppt over a six-month study), and the town's landfill leachate separately measured 18,540 ppt [19][21]. No Tasman-specific PFAS test result could be confirmed; Hartland's own residential wells (n=2) both tested below 20 ppt, so a figure of 1,650 ng/L, if real, most likely reflects a non-residential medium (effluent, surface water, or soil) — **Unresolved** as stated.
- **Former Loring AFB / Limestone (claimed max 340 ng/L):** Corroborated. Loring is a well-documented former Strategic Air Command base with extensive AFFF use; 2018 Air Force testing found on-base PFOS up to 8,770–11,000 ppt, and Maine's statewide well table shows 73 Limestone-area wells sampled with 6 exceeding the 20 ppt standard, consistent with a contamination footprint extending off-base at lower concentrations [22][23]. The claimed maximum of 340 ng/L is far below the documented on-base extreme and is plausible for an off-base or lower-concentration monitoring point.

## F4 — Sampling design confound

**Verdict:** Corroborated — yes, Maine's PFAS sampling is risk-targeted, not random, and this is a genuine confound for any facility co-location analysis.

EGAD (Environmental and Geographic Analysis Database) is Maine DEP's central repository for site, soil, sediment, air, and water-quality data tied to actual and potential contamination sources statewide, holding tens of thousands of PFAS records across several hundred sites [24]. The Water Quality Portal (WQP) is a national aggregator (USGS NWIS + EPA STORET + USDA STEWARDS + 400+ state/tribal/local programs) covering over 250 million discrete water-quality results drawn from a mix of compliance monitoring, ambient/trend networks, and targeted investigations [25]. Maine's flagship PFAS effort is explicitly and legally risk-targeted: Public Law 2021 c.478 restricts DEP's investigation to "locations associated with a source or suspected source of PFAS," and DEP prioritized sludge/septage land-application sites into tiers using historical licensing records rather than drawing a probability sample of the landscape [27][26]. DEP's own commissioner acknowledged the resulting ascertainment bias directly, stating "I can't help but suspect that we may appear to have a bigger problem, in part, because we have been proactive in looking for it" [17]. Because sampling intensity and site selection are themselves driven by suspected proximity to industrial/military sources, any analysis that co-locates detections with EPA-flagged facilities using this dataset will tend to overstate the facility–detection association relative to what a randomly sited monitoring network would show — the confound the question anticipates is real and should be disclosed as a limitation.

## F5 — Predicted functional use

**Verdict:** Partially corroborated

EPA's "Predicted Functional Use" is not an ad hoc label — it descends from a peer-reviewed method. Phillips et al. (2017) built 41 quantitative structure–use relationship (QSUR) random-forest classifiers on EPA's Functional Use (FUse/CPDat) database, using ToxPrint structural descriptors to predict OECD-harmonized function categories (including surfactant-, foamer-, and emulsion-stabilizer-type categories) for chemicals lacking documented use data; balanced random forests were used specifically because harmonized-function training sets are small and class-imbalanced, and valid models were achievable for only 41 of 49 candidate categories [28]. These QSUR models are the direct basis of the "Predicted Probability of Functional Use" field in the CompTox Dashboard and ICE's Functional Use Explorer [29][30][31]. Critically, EPA maintains a hard distinction between **curated** functional use (extracted from actual product/SDS/patent documentation via the Factotum pipeline) and **predicted** functional use (a QSAR inference from structural similarity to chemicals with known use); the latter is explicitly a lower-confidence, analogy-based estimate, not a measured or reported use [32]. Relying on five predicted-only categories with zero curated OECD backing for any PFAS in the set is therefore defensible as a hypothesis-generating signal — the underlying models are published and validated on held-out data — but is **not** defensible as a confirmed, mechanistic attribution of what these specific compounds were actually used for; it should be presented and weighted as lower-confidence than facility co-location evidence, and the absence of any curated assignment for the compound set should be stated explicitly as a limitation rather than implied.

## F6 — Contradicting evidence

**Verdict:** Corroborated — the literature contains substantial, specific evidence that facility proximity alone is an incomplete, and at times misleading, proxy for PFAS source attribution.

**Atmospheric deposition** decouples contamination from a facility's direct water discharge: airborne PFAS/GenX from the Chemours Fayetteville Works fluoropolymer plant has been modeled and measured contaminating private wells, household dust, and produce for miles downwind via deposition rather than effluent, and a comparable airborne pathway was documented at Saint-Gobain's Merrimack, NH plant; PFAS is also measurably present in precipitation generally, providing a diffuse background independent of any single mapped facility [34][35][36]. **Biosolids/septage land application** is arguably the strongest complicating factor for this specific dataset: Maine's own experience shows PFAS-contaminated sludge trucked and spread on farm fields tens of miles from the originating mill or WWTP, producing contamination hotspots (e.g., Fairfield's wells at 12,910–30,000+ ppt) with no PFAS-relevant facility anywhere nearby — the field, not the mill, is the proximate source, breaking any simple distance-to-facility relationship [17][41][42]. **Septic systems** are an independently documented, spatially diffuse source: studies on Cape Cod and in Wisconsin found significant PFAS linked to septic discharge (driven by PFAS-containing household cleaners and consumer products) even away from industrial sites, and a 2025 study found elevated PFAS precursors specifically in residential septage [37][38]. **Groundwater plumes migrate well beyond 1–3 km**: ITRC guidance describes dilute PFAS plumes extending for miles, with more-mobile short-chain PFAS traveling disproportionately farther than long-chain PFAS — meaning a "same-cell" facility may not be the true source of a given detection, while a facility several kilometers away still could be [33]. Finally, **precursor transformation** actively degrades the reliability of simple chemical or spatial fingerprints: fluorotelomer- and sulfonamido-based AFFF precursors transform during transport into terminal perfluoroalkyl acids, shifting analyte ratios over distance and time — precisely why forensic source-attribution researchers have had to develop specialized tools (the TOP assay, isomer-ratio analysis) rather than relying on raw concentration or proximity alone [39][40]. None of this refutes the general proximity effect corroborated in F1, but it does mean that any single cell's specific attribution to its nearest flagged facility should be treated as probabilistic, not proof.
