# Literature comparison — Environmental Justice across U.S. counties

Per-claim concordance of this study's headline findings against the primary literature, retrieved via the **PubMed** MCP connector. Concordance uses the closed six-term set (SUPPORTED / PARTIALLY SUPPORTED / CONTRADICTED / MIXED / NOVEL / UNRESOLVED). Verdicts were assessed against PubMed abstracts and indexed metadata; none required full-text retrieval beyond the abstract. Every `[n]` resolves to the reference list at the foot of this document (identical entries to report §12).

---

### Claim 1 — Cumulative burden concentrates in a Lower Mississippi Valley / Deep South belt plus legacy-industrial cities.
**Concordance: SUPPORTED.** Index-based frameworks that combine multiple environmental burdens with social vulnerability to identify "hotspots" recover exactly this kind of disproportionately-burdened geography, and endorse mapping cumulative burden as corroboration across layers rather than a single measure [3][4]. Regional U.S. work reports the same Southern concentration of exposure-plus-disadvantage [7]. Our county ranking (Wayne MI, Caddo LA, St. Louis, Baltimore, and a Louisiana/Mississippi/Arkansas/Oklahoma belt) is consistent with these hotspot geographies. Caveat: the cited hotspot studies are sub-national/intra-urban; the concordance is methodological and geographic, not a county-for-county replication.

### Claim 2 — Combining multiple environmental burdens with social vulnerability into corroborated hot-spots is a valid EJ method.
**Concordance: SUPPORTED.** The "hotspot" index literature explicitly builds composite exposure indices and overlays social vulnerability [3][4]; chemical-risk guidance further recommends treating combined chemical **and non-chemical** (social/economic) stressors together and *not* collapsing susceptibility into a single default factor [5] — which matches our decision to preserve evidence types and score by consensus rather than one fused index.

### Claim 3 — The burden↔service mismatch peaks in rural, high-poverty (Black Belt) counties with provider shortages.
**Concordance: SUPPORTED.** Rural Southern communities show persistent, place-based health disparities and structural barriers to care (the "location dynamics" of rural disadvantage) [6], and segregated Southern counties face disproportionate environmental exposure [7]. Both support our finding that the highest burden + lowest service-capacity counties are rural, high-poverty, and concentrated in the Black Belt (Macon GA, Wilcox AL, Quitman MS).

### Claim 4 — Measured PFAS (PFOS/PFOA) act via PPARα to drive hepatic lipid dysregulation and steatosis (AOP-Wiki AOP 529).
**Concordance: SUPPORTED.** A NHANES + multi-model integrative study reports a significant association between PFAS exposure and hepatic steatosis/NAFLD and identifies **PPARα activation by PFOA/PFOS as the critical molecular initiating event**, with downstream ACOX1-mediated peroxisomal β-oxidation disruption [1] — a direct corroboration of the AOP 529 chain (PPAR binding → lipid dysregulation → liver steatosis) we surfaced from AOP-Wiki. Developmental PFOS exposure likewise perturbs lipid uptake/metabolism via PPAR signalling in zebrafish [2]. Caveat: mechanistic/associational evidence, not proof of county-level disease causation.

### Claim 5 — Residential segregation co-occurs with elevated air-toxicant exposure and respiratory disparities.
**Concordance: SUPPORTED.** In Oklahoma, facilities in African-American–segregated **rural** counties were ~9× more likely (and Hispanic-segregated urban counties ~5×) to face high ambient respiratory-toxicant exposure [7] — direct support for the segregation↔exposure↔respiratory-disparity link, and consistent with our Deep South / Oklahoma high-burden cluster and elevated asthma/COPD prevalence there.

### Claim 6 — County-level industrial-facility *counts* are decoupled from (even inversely related to) social/health burden.
**Concordance: MIXED.** The environmental-justice literature robustly finds polluting facilities *concentrating* in socially vulnerable communities — the opposite sign — but at **sub-county** (tract, block-group, facility-buffer) resolution with population-normalised density [5][7]. Our inverse **county-scale** correlation (ρ = −0.51 with public-safety burden) is a **count-vs-rate / population** artifact: facility counts track county population, pulling toward well-resourced metros. The literature and the KG are measuring different things at different grains, so this is a scope difference, not a contradiction; it motivates the sub-county, density-normalised re-analysis named as this study's testable prediction.
